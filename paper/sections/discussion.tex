\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Implications for practice.} Four suggestions follow from the tables, within the limits below. (i) Check the selected strategy's returns for autocorrelation before any multiple-testing adjustment. When it is present, deflate the $t$-statistic for it, with Lo's factor for autoregressive decay or a heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent variance \citep{newey1987simple} otherwise, and expect a slightly liberal test when the true autocorrelation is zero. (ii) Compare corrections at matched sides and at the same number of resamples; otherwise the comparison measures conventions. (iii) Treat $\mathrm{DSR}\ge 0.95$ as the demanding rule it is: it tests whether the best strategy beats the expected best of $N$ skill-less ones, not whether it has any skill, and over two years of daily data it will pass few strategies with a true Sharpe ratio of 2. (iv) When returns are markedly skewed or fat-tailed, do not rely on any single correction in this benchmark; check the chosen test's size on simulated searches shaped like one's own returns, or use a studentized block bootstrap \citep{ledoit2008robust}, which v0 does not score.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The benchmark covers one search shape ($N=\nzTrials$, $T=504$) and one alternative (a single strategy with a Sharpe ratio of 2). Size distortions from skew and autocorrelation depend on $T$ and $N$ in ways the benchmark does not map, and real searches often contain many weakly skilled strategies rather than one strongly skilled one. Each family changes one feature at a time; real returns combine several. The only dependence studied is AR(1), which is the Lo factor's best case. The resampling methods most used for data snooping---the Reality Check \citep{white2000reality}, the test for superior predictive ability \citep{hansen2005spa} and the stepwise procedure of \citet{romano2005stepwise}---resample all strategies jointly and preserve their dependence; v0 does not score them, and its one bootstrap resamples only the selected strategy. The DSR is checked only at its 0.95 point, not across its range as a probability. Finally, a benchmark designed by the author of one of the scored methods cannot be neutral in the sense of \citet{bartos2025living}. We publish every generator, validator and seed, and four of the seven validators call the same code a user of the public implementations runs, but independent replication with independently chosen families would be more convincing than anything we can do ourselves.

\paragraph{Next versions.} The benchmark is versioned so that it can grow without rewriting its history. Natural additions are families that combine features (skewed and autocorrelated returns) or have non-AR dependence, a grid over $N$ and $T$, alternatives with many weakly skilled strategies, block and studentized bootstraps, joint resampling tests of the Reality Check family, and an estimator of the effective number of trials for correlated searches.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
The corrections practitioners use for backtest overfitting keep their promises in the setting they were derived for and fail in predictable ways outside it. Autocorrelation was the most damaging violation tested: it raised the false-positive rate of every test that ignores it, other than the near-zero DSR, about fourfold, a naive bootstrap included, and Lo's factor repaired it in the AR(1) case at a small cost elsewhere. Skew and fat tails were the most persistent: no validator in the benchmark handled both, in part because selection itself distorts the moments of the chosen series. Several apparent differences between methods come from how they are read rather than from the methods: the haircut's conservatism comes from its two-sided convention, the deflated Sharpe ratio's low power from its 0.95 rule, and the bootstrap's from its number of resamples. An open benchmark with known ground truth makes such statements checkable, and anyone can rerun or extend it from the published code and seeds.
