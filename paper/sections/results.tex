\subsection{The baseline is calibrated}
Under independent normal returns the luck-equivalent-trials test is exact by construction: the $t$-statistic is Student $t$ and the trials are independent. Its size is \nzLuckTrialsIidNormalSizeExt\% in the robustness run; the published run's \nzLuckTrialsIidNormalSizeVzero\% lies inside that run's 99\% band (\nzBandVzeroLo--\nzBandVzeroHi\%) and is consistent with sampling noise. The non-normal-error version (\nzLuckTrialsNonnormalSeIidNormalSizeExt\%), the one-sided haircut (\nzHaircutBonferroniIidNormalSizeExt\%) and the bootstrap (\nzBootstrapBestIidNormalSizeExt\%) are also calibrated here. The Lo-corrected version is slightly liberal (\nzLuckTrialsLoIidNormalSizeExt\%, just above the band), for a reason discussed below, and the deflated Sharpe ratio rejects almost nothing (\nzDeflatedSharpeIidNormalSizeExt\%).

\subsection{Autocorrelation breaks the tests that ignore it}
With a lag-one autocorrelation of 0.2, the tests that treat returns as independent rejected about four times as often as their level: \nzLuckTrialsArOneSizeExt\% for the luck-equivalent-trials test, \nzHaircutBonferroniArOneSizeExt\% for the one-sided haircut, \nzLuckTrialsNonnormalSeArOneSizeExt\% with the non-normal standard error and \nzBootstrapBestArOneSizeExt\% for the i.i.d.\ bootstrap. The deflated Sharpe ratio, which also ignores autocorrelation, stays far below its level (\nzDeflatedSharpeArOneSizeExt\%) because it rejects almost nothing anywhere. Positive autocorrelation makes the sample Sharpe ratio more variable than the i.i.d.\ formula assumes, so the $t$-statistic is too large. The non-normal correction adjusts for the shape of the marginal distribution, which is normal here, and cannot help. The i.i.d.\ bootstrap resamples single observations and so destroys the very dependence that inflates the Sharpe ratio. Block and stationary bootstraps \citep{kunsch1989jackknife,politis1994stationary}, and the studentized circular block bootstrap that \citet{ledoit2008robust} propose for Sharpe ratios, are the natural resampling remedies; v0 does not include them.

Deflating the $t$-statistic by the autocorrelation-adjusted annualisation factor of \citet{lo2002sharpe} brings the size to \nzLuckTrialsLoArOneSizeExt\% for the luck-equivalent-trials test and \nzHaircutBonferroniLoArOneSizeExt\% for the haircut. That is inside the 99\% band but at its upper edge (the 95\% band ends at \nzBandExtNinetyFiveHi\%). The result should not be over-read, for three reasons.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The AR(1) family satisfies the factor's assumption of geometric autocorrelation decay exactly, so it is the correction's best case. Dependence of other shapes---a moving average, higher-order autoregression, long memory---would call for a heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent variance \citep{newey1987simple} or a block resampling test, which v0 does not score.
  \item The correction is not free elsewhere. In the seven families without autocorrelation it raised the size by between \nzLoShiftMin\ and \nzLoShiftMax\ percentage points. Mainly, the estimated factor adds noise to the $t$-statistic, which fattens its upper tail even when the true autocorrelation is zero; slightly, the factor is convex in the estimate, so an estimate of $-0.05$ raises the $t$-statistic by more than one of $+0.05$ lowers it.
  \item Its power under autocorrelation (\nzLuckTrialsLoArOnePowerExt\%) is well below the uncorrected test's (\nzLuckTrialsArOnePowerExt\%), but the uncorrected test's power is bought with a fourfold excess of false positives. Size-adjusted, the two are close (\nzLuckTrialsLoArOneAdjPowerExt\% and \nzLuckTrialsArOneAdjPowerExt\%; Table~\ref{tab:power-adj}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The haircut read one-sided is the \v{S}id\'ak test}
\citet{harvey2015backtesting} define the haircut with two-sided $p$-values. For a positive Sharpe ratio, a two-sided $p\le 0.05$ is a one-sided test at 2.5\%, which is why the haircut columns of Table~\ref{tab:size-v0} sit near half the level (\nzHaircutBonferroniIidNormalSizeExtTwoSided\% under i.i.d.\ normal returns in the robustness run). Halving the $p$-value is not how Harvey and Liu define their method; we do it only to compare tests at a common level. Read that way, the haircut and the luck-equivalent-trials test are, analytically, nearly the same test. Both use the same Student $t$ tail $p_1$ of the same statistic; Bonferroni rejects when $Np_1 \le 0.05$, \v{S}id\'ak when $1-(1-p_1)^N\le 0.05$, and the two disagree only when $p_1$ falls between \nzBonfPOne\ and \nzSidakPOne. In the simulations they disagreed in at most \nzLetHcMaxDiff\ of \nzRepsExt\ searches in any cell. This is a known equivalence, not an empirical finding; its practical consequence is that comparisons of these corrections at unmatched sides measure the choice of sides. The benchmark's seven columns therefore contain five distinct decision rules: the \v{S}id\'ak--Bonferroni $t$ test, the same test with the Lo factor, the non-normal-error variant, the deflated Sharpe ratio and the bootstrap.

\subsection{Skew and fat tails}
The Student-$t$ tests are liberal under negative skew (\nzLuckTrialsSkewNegativeSizeExt\% for the luck-equivalent-trials test, \nzHaircutBonferroniSkewNegativeSizeExt\% for the one-sided haircut) and conservative under positive skew (\nzLuckTrialsSkewPositiveSizeExt\%). With $T=504$, the $t$-statistic of a skewed series is itself skewed in finite samples, so one of its tails is heavier than Student $t$ and the other lighter.

The non-normal standard error of \citet{mertens2002comments} moves the error rather than removing it: it is conservative under negative skew (\nzLuckTrialsNonnormalSeSkewNegativeSizeExt\%) and clearly liberal under symmetric fat tails (\nzLuckTrialsNonnormalSeStudentTFourSizeExt\%). Under positive skew it was outside the band in the published run (\nzLuckTrialsNonnormalSeSkewPositiveSizeVzero\%) and in a first 10,000-replication run with the same design (\nzNNSkewPosSizeExtOne\%), but inside it in the robustness run reported here (\nzLuckTrialsNonnormalSeSkewPositiveSizeExt\%); it is liberal there more often than not. It is not designed to correct the null distribution. Under the null of a zero Sharpe ratio its variance term equals one for every i.i.d.\ distribution; skewness and kurtosis enter only because the term is evaluated at the estimated Sharpe ratio. Selection then makes that estimate misleading. Under $t_4$ returns the selected best series has a mean sample skewness of \nzSkewBestStudentTFour, against \nzSkewOthersStudentTFour\ for the other series (Table~\ref{tab:diagnostics}): a series tends to win because it had a large positive outlier. Plugging that positive skewness into the formula shrinks the winner's standard error, and the $t_4$ distribution's infinite fourth moment makes the kurtosis term unstable as well.

The bootstrap is within the band under negative skew (\nzBootstrapBestSkewNegativeSizeExt\%), conservative under positive skew (\nzBootstrapBestSkewPositiveSizeExt\%) and liberal under fat tails (\nzBootstrapBestStudentTFourSizeExt\%). The last is plausibly the same selection effect: resampling a winner that owes its rank to an outlier reproduces the outlier's influence. No validator in the benchmark is calibrated across all three shapes.

Under GARCH volatility clustering and regime switching every test except the DSR stays within one percentage point of 5\% (Table~\ref{tab:size-ext}); the flagged cells are the non-normal-error test under regimes (\nzLuckTrialsNonnormalSeRegimesSizeExt\%, liberal) and, marginally, the \v{S}id\'ak test, the one-sided haircut and the bootstrap under GARCH (\nzHaircutBonferroniGarchSizeExt--\nzBootstrapBestGarchSizeExt\%, conservative; the \v{S}id\'ak test was \nzLetGarchSizeExtOne\% in the first 10,000-replication run). In both families returns remain a martingale difference sequence with finite variance, so the $t$-test is asymptotically valid, and two years of daily data are enough for it to be nearly so here.

\subsection{Correlated trials make the Bonferroni-type tests conservative}
When every pair of strategies has correlation 0.5, the search contains fewer than \nzTrials\ effectively independent trials, and every test that assumes \nzTrials\ is conservative: \nzLuckTrialsCorrelatedTrialsSizeExt\% for the luck-equivalent-trials test, \nzHaircutBonferroniCorrelatedTrialsSizeExt\% for the haircut and \nzBootstrapBestCorrelatedTrialsSizeExt\% for the bootstrap. The unused level costs power: \nzLuckTrialsCorrelatedTrialsPowerExt\% raw against \nzLuckTrialsCorrelatedTrialsAdjPowerExt\% size-adjusted. An estimate of the effective number of trials would recover it; v0 does not include one.

\subsection{The deflated Sharpe ratio at 0.95 tests a harder hypothesis}
\citet{bailey2014dsr} accept a strategy when $\mathrm{DSR}\ge 0.95$. Scored this way, the deflated Sharpe ratio rejects at most \nzDeflatedSharpeCorrelatedTrialsSizeExt\% of skill-less searches (in the correlated family, and at most \nzDsrMaxSizeExceptCorr\% elsewhere). Under i.i.d.\ normal returns it fails to accept \nzDsrFailToAccept\% of searches that contain a strategy with a true annualized Sharpe ratio of 2 over two years (between \nzDsrFailToAcceptMin\% and \nzDsrFailToAcceptMax\% across families; power \nzDeflatedSharpeIidNormalPowerExt\% under i.i.d.\ normal returns, against \nzLuckTrialsIidNormalPowerExt\% for the \v{S}id\'ak test).

Formally, the rule $\mathrm{DSR}\ge 0.95$ tests $H_0\colon SR\le SR_0$, where $SR_0$ is the expected maximum of $N$ skill-less trials, a harder hypothesis than $H_0\colon SR=0$ for the best of $N$. \citet{bailey2014dsr} themselves read the DSR as the probability that the true Sharpe ratio exceeds zero; read that way, a value of at least 0.95 should occur in about 5\% of skill-less searches, and it occurred in at most \nzDeflatedSharpeCorrelatedTrialsSizeExt\%, so at its 0.95 point it is strongly conservative. The difference in hurdles is large:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Under independence and normality, the one-sided 5\% critical value for the best of $N=\nzTrials$ trials is about \nzSidakCrit\ standard errors.
  \item The DSR's benchmark is about \nzExpMax\ cross-trial standard deviations: the approximation of \citet{bailey2014pseudo}; the exact expected maximum of 20 standard normals is \nzExpMaxExact. The rule then asks for a further \nzSingleCrit\ standard errors of confidence on top of that.
  \item The cross-trial standard deviation is computed from all \nzTrials\ strategies, including the best. Under the null it averages \nzSdRatioNullIidNormal\ times the i.i.d.\ standard error, which gives a hurdle of about \nzDsrHurdleNull\ standard errors. Under the alternative the skilled strategy widens the dispersion to \nzSdRatioSkillIidNormal\ times the standard error, and the hurdle to about \nzDsrHurdleSkill: a skilled strategy raises its own benchmark.
  \item The comparison assumes a dispersion near the standard error. When trials are strongly correlated the dispersion shrinks, and below \nzSdRatioCrossover\ times the standard error the DSR's hurdle falls under the \v{S}id\'ak value; in the correlated family it is \nzSdRatioNullCorrelatedTrials\ under the null, which lowers the hurdle to about \nzDsrHurdleNullCorr\ standard errors, close to the \v{S}id\'ak value, and is why the DSR rejects most there.
\end{itemize}
The ranking the DSR induces is better than its raw power suggests. Size-adjusted, its power under i.i.d.\ normal returns is \nzDeflatedSharpeIidNormalAdjPowerExt\%, against \nzLuckTrialsIidNormalAdjPowerExt\% for the \v{S}id\'ak test, and across families it trails by \nzDsrAdjGapMin--\nzDsrAdjGapMax\ points (Table~\ref{tab:power-adj}). The 0.95 threshold costs most of the power; the statistic, whose benchmark moves with the dispersion of the whole search, costs the rest.

\subsection{Resolution matters for the bootstrap}
\label{sec:bootres}
A bootstrap $p$-value with $B$ resamples is a Monte Carlo estimate, and a test built on it loses power as $B$ falls \citep{davidson2000bootstrap}. Here the loss is sharp because of the \v{S}id\'ak adjustment. With the published run's 400 resamples the smallest attainable adjusted $p$-value is \nzBootMinPFourHundred, just below the level, so the test rejects only when no resample reaches the observed Sharpe ratio; with \nzBootDraws\ resamples it rejects only when at most \nzBootMaxHitsExt\ do. To measure the effect without run-to-run noise, we scored the same \nzBrReps\ searches per cell with both numbers of resamples, drawing the resamples from separate generator streams. Under i.i.d.\ normal returns the bootstrap's power rises from \nzBrIidNormalPowerBootFourHundred\% to \nzBrIidNormalPowerBootMax\% (the \v{S}id\'ak $t$ test: \nzBrIidNormalPowerLet\%), under $t_4$ returns from \nzBrStudentTFourPowerBootFourHundred\% to \nzBrStudentTFourPowerBootMax\%, and under negative skew from \nzBrSkewNegativePowerBootFourHundred\% to \nzBrSkewNegativePowerBootMax\%, while its size moves by less than half a percentage point (\nzBrIidNormalSizeBootFourHundred\% to \nzBrIidNormalSizeBootMax\% under i.i.d.\ returns). Comparisons of resampling tests with analytical ones should therefore state the number of resamples.

\subsection{Rejecting is not the same as finding}
Table~\ref{tab:correct-ext} in the appendix counts only rejections in which the selected best strategy is the truly skilled one. Under i.i.d.\ normal returns the skilled strategy is the in-sample best in \nzBestSkilledIidNormalExt\% of searches, so even a perfect test would crown the wrong strategy in about one search in five. Autocorrelation widens the gap: the skilled strategy is the best in only \nzBestSkilledArOneExt\% of AR(1) searches.

\subsection{Robustness}
\label{sec:robust}
The published run is deterministic, and running its code reproduces the published result file byte for byte. The robustness run changes only what could make its numbers artefacts:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the generator, to xoshiro128** seeded by splitmix32 \citep{blackman2021scrambled}, whose first outputs we checked against the reference implementation;
  \item the number of replications, from \nzRepsVzero\ to \nzRepsExt\ per cell, with new seeds;
  \item the bootstrap's resolution, from 400 to \nzBootDraws\ resamples;
  \item the sides of the haircut, which is also read one-sided.
\end{itemize}
The families, $N$, $T$, the effect size and every validator except the bootstrap's number of resamples are unchanged, so the run rules out generator and Monte Carlo artefacts, not sensitivity to the design. The qualitative findings of the published run hold, with these differences:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The i.i.d.\ normal size of the luck-equivalent-trials test moves from \nzLuckTrialsIidNormalSizeVzero\% to \nzLuckTrialsIidNormalSizeExt\%.
  \item The bootstrap gains power with more resamples (Section~\ref{sec:bootres}), and it is liberal under $t_4$ returns (\nzBootstrapBestStudentTFourSizeExt\%; \nzBootTFourSizeExtOne\% with 400 resamples in a first 10,000-replication run), which the published run's wider band did not detect (\nzBootstrapBestStudentTFourSizeVzero\%).
  \item The Lo-corrected test's slight excess size under i.i.d.\ returns becomes visible (\nzLuckTrialsLoIidNormalSizeExt\%).
  \item Individual cells near the band edges can move by two or three Monte Carlo standard errors between runs; the non-normal-error test under positive skew is the clearest case. Conclusions in this paper rest on patterns across families and runs, not on single cells.
\end{itemize}
