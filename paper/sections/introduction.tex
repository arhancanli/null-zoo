\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A researcher who tries many variants of a trading strategy and reports the best one reports a biased number. The best of $N$ strategies with no skill has an in-sample Sharpe ratio that grows with $N$, roughly in proportion to $\sqrt{2\ln N}$ times the spread of the individual estimates \citep{bailey2014pseudo}, and the same selection effect pervades published factor research \citep{harvey2016cross}. Two families of corrections are now standard. The first adjusts the best strategy's own statistic: the deflated Sharpe ratio \citep{bailey2014dsr}, built on the probabilistic Sharpe ratio \citep{bailey2012frontier}; the multiple-testing haircut of \citet{harvey2015backtesting}; and family-wise adjustments such as those of \citet{holm1979} and \citet{sidak1967}. The second resamples the whole search: White's Reality Check \citep{white2000reality}, applied to trading rules by \citet{sullivan1999datasnooping}, Hansen's test for superior predictive ability \citep{hansen2005spa} and the stepwise procedures of \citet{romano2005stepwise} and \citet{hsu2010stepwise}.

Each correction rests on assumptions---normal or independent returns, independent trials, large samples---that real searches violate in predictable ways. Returns are fat-tailed, skewed, volatility-clustered and sometimes autocorrelated; strategy variants share most of their positions and so are correlated; and two years of daily data is a short sample for a variance estimate. Inference for a single Sharpe ratio under such violations is well studied \citep{lo2002sharpe,opdyke2007comparing,ledoit2008robust}. How much each violation distorts each \emph{selection} correction, applied to the best of a search as practitioners apply it, is rarely answered in a form a practitioner can look up.

This paper answers it with a simulation benchmark, the \emph{Null Zoo}. It draws complete research searches---\nz{trials} candidate strategies of \nz{obs} daily returns each---from nine return families whose ground truth is known by construction, applies \nz{v1.validator_count} validators as their implementations are meant to be called, and records how often each one declares a skilled strategy when there is none (its \emph{size}) and when one strategy has a true annualized Sharpe ratio of 1, 2 or 3 (its \emph{power}). The main run has \nz{reps.v1} searches per cell. Every governed run was pre-registered before it was run, following recent arguments that comparative simulation studies need the same protection against selective reporting as empirical ones \citep{pawel2024pitfalls,siepe2024simulation}; a surprising result was confirmed on fresh seeds under a new pre-registration before it was used, and its explanation was tested by an independent implementation in a second language.

The findings:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Hansen's studentized SPA test, and StepM with it, is oversized at this horizon; White's Reality Check is not.} At a nominal 5\%, the consistent SPA test rejected \nz{both.size_range.spa_consistent}\% of skill-less searches across families and runs, the Reality Check \nz{both.size_range.reality_check}\%.
  \item \textbf{The cause is a variance estimate the bootstrap holds fixed.} The SPA statistic divides each mean by a long-run standard deviation estimated once and reused in every resample, so the bootstrap ignores the estimate's noise; under negative skew the mean and its estimated standard deviation are also dependent. Re-studentizing inside the bootstrap removes the excess (\nz{v2.A.size_range_nonar.spa_c_boot_t}\% outside the autocorrelated family), as Hall and Wilson's guideline would predict \citep{hall1991guidelines}. The excess shrinks with the sample (\nz{v2.B.size.spa_c.iid_normal.n252_k20}\% with one year of i.i.d.\ data, \nz{v2.B.size.spa_c.iid_normal.n2520_k20}\% with ten), grows with the number of strategies and with the block length, and reaches \nz{v2.A.size.spa_c_b22.skew_negative}\% under negative skew at the block length the Python package arch uses by default.
  \item \textbf{arch's SPA test stays close to its level because it does not studentize.} In arch \nz{v2.D.arch} \citep{sheppard2025arch} the SPA test's \texttt{studentize} option has no effect on the result; a pending change \citep{arch871} would, on our implementation of its formula, make it oversized at two years of daily data.
  \item \textbf{Studentizing and recentring still pay, when done right.} When volatilities differ, the unstudentized Reality Check almost never finds a skilled low-volatility strategy (power \nz{v2.C.power.rc.heterogeneous_volatility}\% against \nz{v2.C.power.spa_c_boot_t.heterogeneous_volatility}\% for the bootstrap-$t$ SPA test); when most strategies are clearly poor, consistent recentring raises power from \nz{v2.C.power.rc.poor_alternatives}\% to \nz{v2.C.power.spa_c_unstud.poor_alternatives}\%. A floored SPA statistic with a strict inequality can reject when no strategy beats the benchmark; counting ties fixes it.
  \item \textbf{Autocorrelation breaks the single-series tests that ignore it.} A lag-one autocorrelation of 0.2 raises their false-positive rate to about \nz{v1.size.luck_trials.ar1}\%; Lo's factor removes the excess in that case, and kernel and block-bootstrap remedies reduce it.
  \item \textbf{The deflated Sharpe ratio with its authors' 0.95 rule tests a harder hypothesis than ``no skill''} and fails to accept \nz{v1.dsr_fail_to_accept.iid_normal}\% of i.i.d.\ searches containing a strategy with a true Sharpe ratio of 2 over two years; most of that is the threshold, not the statistic.
  \item \textbf{Selection, not correction, sets the ceiling.} An oracle that knows which strategy is skilled rejects in \nz{v1.power2.oracle_hac_t.iid_normal}\% of such searches; the best calibrated best-of-\nz{trials} tests reach about \nz{v1.power2.luck_trials.iid_normal}\%.
\end{enumerate}
The mechanism in finding 2---a noisy long-run variance inflating a studentized test---is familiar from tests of equal forecast accuracy \citep{diebold1995comparing,harvey1997testing}. What the benchmark adds is its size where strategy research operates, its interaction with the maximum over strategies and with skewness, a remedy that keeps the benefits of studentizing, and its consequences for widely used code.

The benchmark scores a statistic introduced by its own author, the \emph{luck-equivalent-trials} count, and the v1 runs were also used to choose a default test for the author's open-source software. \citet{bartos2025living} argue that simulation studies designed by a method's developers carry a conflict of interest. We report that conflict, pre-register the runs, publish every generator, validator, seed and result file, and note that the benchmark's clearest failures---autocorrelation, and the floored strict-inequality $p$-value---apply to the author's own implementations as much as to anyone's.

\paragraph{Related work.}
\citet{arian2024backtest} compare out-of-sample testing schemes---walk-forward, $k$-fold, purged and combinatorial purged cross-validation---in a synthetic market with known structure, scoring them by the probability of backtest overfitting \citep{bailey2017pbo} and the deflated Sharpe ratio. Their object is the scheme that produces a backtest; ours is the correction applied to its result. \citet{santoni2026minerva} combine the deflated Sharpe ratio, the probability of backtest overfitting, the SPA test and the minimum track-record length into one robustness grade and report its discrimination on synthetic markets; they do not report the size of the individual components at a nominal level, which our results suggest matters for the SPA component in particular. \citet{hsu2010stepwise} and \citet{hansen2005spa} evaluate the SPA test and its stepwise extension by simulation in their own designs, and \citet{frederiksen2026heavy} study predictive-ability tests when losses have infinite variance. The size and power of multiple-testing procedures under dependence are well studied in statistics; our contribution is a small, open, versioned and pre-registered benchmark that uses the return shapes and search structures of trading research and calls each correction the way it is called in practice.
